## 6.1 Outdoor Settling Ponds

**What they are:** Large concrete block-walled open basins outdoors.  
**There are two distinct ponds:**

| Pond | Typical water appearance | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary settling pond** | Turbid green or grey | First pond receiving raw site water. Heavy solids settle here. |
| **Stabilization / secondary pond** | Dark red or purple (iron-rich) | Receives more heavily contaminated water. Oil/water separation and additional settling. |

**What they do:** Gravity pre-settling. Given time (hours to days), large solids sink to the bottom, free oil floats to the surface, and the clearer middle layer is drawn off for further treatment.

`[CHECK]` Each shift:
- [ ] Water level in each pond — note if rising or falling
- [ ] Oil sheen on the surface — any visible oily rainbow or dark film?
- [ ] Debris (trash, vegetation) blocking the outlet pipe?
- [ ] Are the concrete block walls intact — any erosion or instability?

`ℹ️ WHY:` The settling ponds are the first treatment step and absorb the "shock loads" from rain events and site activity. If they're overloaded or short-circuiting, the rest of the system gets hammered.

### Why backwashing is necessary
Over time, water flowing through the sand carves the same paths over and over, forming **channels** — narrow worn-in routes that water flows through easily while bypassing the rest of the sand bed. Once channels form, the filter looks like it's running fine (water still flows) but it's actually filtering less and less, because most of the water is taking the path of least resistance instead of passing through clean sand. Backwashing (reversing the flow) breaks up those channels and resets the sand bed. Beyond backwashing, the sand itself also needs to be fully replaced periodically as it gets dirty — how often depends on how much solids loading it sees.

### When does it need backwashing?
Backwash the sand filter when any of the following:
1. Outlet turbidity starts climbing above your target
2. Flow rate through the filter drops significantly (filter is clogged)
3. Pressure drop across the filter exceeds the setpoint

### Backwash procedure (generic — confirm exact valve positions with supervisor)
`[ACTION]`  
1. Close the inlet valve.  
2. Close the outlet (forward flow) valve.  
3. Open the backwash drain valve.  
4. Open the backwash supply valve (clean water in from the bottom, up through the sand).  
5. Run until the backwash discharge runs clear — typically 5–15 minutes.  
6. Close backwash valves, re-open inlet and outlet to return to normal operation.  
7. Redirect first 5–10 minutes of post-backwash effluent back to the wet cell (not forward to clean storage) — filter needs to "ripen" after backwash.  
8. Record backwash in the log: time, reason, duration.

## 6.5 Carbon Vessel (SRS-CV-15)

**Appearance:** Blue cylindrical pressure vessel, "PV SERIES" label, tagged "SRS-CV-15". Sits on a wooden pallet. **Maximum working pressure: 75 PSIG** (marked on the vessel — never exceed this). Matches photo IMG_2796 (yellow "...SERIES" tag visible, hose fitting at base, mounted against the wood-stud wall).

`ℹ️ CONFIRMED — currently in active use:` operator confirms the sand filter is bypassed (Section 6.4), so water currently flows straight from settling to the **bag filter, then this carbon vessel** — this is one of the two filtration steps the water actually sees right now.

**What it does:** Activated carbon adsorption. The vessel is filled with granular activated carbon (GAC) — a highly porous material with enormous surface area. Dissolved petroleum hydrocarbons, colour compounds, and odour-causing organics bind to the carbon surface as water passes through.

`ℹ️ WHY:` Suspended solids are removed by sand filtration. But dissolved hydrocarbons (the ones that give the water a fuel smell or create a downstream sheen) cannot be filtered — they pass through sand. Carbon adsorption is the only way to remove them.

### Normal operation checks
`[CHECK]`  
- [ ] Water is flowing through (inlet valve open, no unusual pressure)
- [ ] Outlet water does not smell of petroleum or fuel
- [ ] No colour in the effluent (should be essentially clear)
- [ ] **CONFIRMED BY OPERATOR:** check turbidity here with the NTU meter (the bag filter and carbon vessel outlets are the site's turbidity check points — see Section 4.3)

### Check frequency — CONFIRMED BY OPERATOR
**Check the carbon vessel every 1.5 months.**

### Signs the carbon is exhausted (needs replacing)
- Petroleum odour returns in the treated water output
- Colour (brown/yellow tint) appears in the effluent
- The vessel has been in service for its rated operational volume (*(confirm with supervisor)* — carbon life depends on hydrocarbon loading)

`⚠️ WARNING:` Backwashing carbon does **not** restore its adsorption capacity. Once the carbon is chemically saturated (it's adsorbed all it can), no amount of backwashing brings that back — the media must be physically removed and replaced with fresh carbon. Exhausted carbon is a regulated waste — dispose of through an approved waste contractor. Backwashing below is a separate, routine maintenance step — see why below.

### Backwash frequency — CONFIRMED BY OPERATOR
**Backwash the carbon vessel every week.** This is separate from the 1.5-month check above: the weekly backwash is routine bed maintenance, not a check for exhaustion.

`ℹ️ WHY (general principle):` Even though backwashing can't "recharge" spent carbon, it still matters — water flowing through a carbon bed over time packs fines and trapped solids into the same flow paths, the same channeling problem described for the sand filter (Section 6.4). A weekly backwash fluffs the bed back up, breaks up channels, and flushes out accumulated debris so the full bed surface stays in contact with the water — keeping the carbon that's still active working as effectively as possible until it's actually exhausted and needs replacing.

### Backwash procedure (generic — confirm exact valve positions/timing with supervisor)
`[ACTION]`
1. Close the inlet (forward flow) valve.
2. Close the outlet valve.
3. Open the backwash drain valve.
4. Open the backwash supply valve (clean water in from the bottom, up through the carbon bed, lifting and expanding it).
5. Run until the backwash discharge runs clear of fines/debris.
6. Close backwash valves, re-open inlet and outlet to return to normal operation.
7. Record the backwash in the log: time, duration.

### How to change a filter bag
`[ACTION]`  
1. **Close the inlet and outlet valves** on the bag filter housing.  
2. Open the **pressure relief/vent valve** to release pressure (there will be water in the housing — have a bucket ready).  
3. Undo the top closure ring and lift the lid.  
4. Pull the old bag out by the collar — hold it over a bucket, it will drip.  
5. Inspect the old bag: How much solids are on it? Is it iron-coloured or hydrocarbon-coloured? Note in the logbook.  
6. Place old bag in waste container.  
7. Install new bag — ensure the collar seats fully in the housing ring. A poorly seated collar allows water to bypass the bag entirely.  
8. Replace lid and tighten closure ring.

3. **Disinfects** — kills bacteria and other pathogens

## 6.9 Oil/Water Separator (Shell Canada System)

**System:** Above-ground separator vessel + underground holding tank  
**Documentation:** Shell Canada log sheet and weekly inspection flow chart on file

**What it does:** Petroleum products (oil, fuel) are less dense than water, so they float. The separator allows water to sit long enough for:
- Petroleum to rise and accumulate at the top (where it is skimmed off)
- Sludge and heavy solids to sink to the bottom
- Cleaner water to be drawn from the middle layer

The recovered petroleum and sludge are **regulated wastes** — they must be drummed, labelled, and removed by a licensed waste contractor. They do NOT go to the discharge point.

### Weekly Inspection (per Shell Canada Flow Chart)

`[ACTION]` Each week:  
1. **Inspect the slab and drainage points** — any spills or leaks visible?  
2. **Dip the underground holding tank** for fuel thickness and sludge thickness.  
   - Record: total liquid depth, fuel thickness, sludge thickness  
3. **Check that liquid level is within acceptable range:** 0.8m to 1.1m from tank bottom (25,000 L = 0.8m, 44,000 L = 1.1m from bottom — *(confirm your tank specs)*)  
4. **Dip the above-ground separator** for fuel and sludge thickness.

### Action thresholds

| Measurement | Action |
|---|---|
| Underground tank sludge > 15 cm | Call to arrange sludge removal |
| Underground tank fuel (L.P. petroleum) > 2.5 cm | Arrange skimming |
| Above-ground separator fuel > 15 cm | Skim manually (call maintenance contractor if it re-occurs) |
| Liquid level outside 0.8–1.1m range | Check pump switch is set to automatic; call contractor if problem persists |
| Any visible petroleum bypass at outlet | Inspect separator; skim; notify Shell field engineer if re-occurs |

### Completing the Shell Canada log sheet

## 6.11 Kontek APC5 Filter Press (Sludge Dewatering)

**Manufacturer:** Kontek Ecology Systems Inc., 3250 Harvester Road Unit #1, Burlington, Ontario L7N 3W8  
**Phone:** (905) 332-8365 | www.kontekecology.com  
**Model:** APC5 | **Serial:** K2012190 | **Electrical:** 120V, 1-phase, 3A

**What it does:** A filter press squeezes water out of sludge, producing:
1. **Filter cakes** — semi-dry solids that fall into collection bins below the press. These are regulated waste — removed by licensed contractor.
2. **Filtrate water** — the water pressed out of the sludge. Usually returned to the head of the treatment system for re-treatment.

`ℹ️ WHY:` The wet cell and settling ponds accumulate sludge continuously. You cannot just pump sludge to a drain — it must be dewatered and disposed of as regulated waste. The filter press is how you turn a liquid sludge into a manageable solid waste.

### Control Panel
- **Keyed ON/OFF switch** (top) — must be in the ON (I) position for the press to operate
- **Green START button** (middle) — starts the hydraulic pump / pressing cycle
- **Red RESET button** (bottom) — stops the cycle / resets after a fault

### Basic Operation Sequence
`[ACTION]`  
**Before starting:**  
1. Confirm all filter plates are properly closed and aligned.  
2. Confirm the hydraulic ram has clamped the plate stack tight (check that there is no gap between plates).  
3. Place collection bins beneath the press to catch filter cakes.  
4. Confirm filtrate collection is set up (where will the pressed water go?).

**Running the press:**  
5. Key switch to ON.  
6. Press the green START button.  
7. Start the sludge feed pump — pump the sludge slurry into the press inlet. The press fills from one end and fills each chamber between plates.  
8. Monitor the filtrate outlets on each plate — you should see water draining from each plate's corner outlets.  
9. The press cycle is complete when: filtrate flow slows to almost nothing (press is full, all water has been squeezed out).  
10. Stop the sludge feed pump.  
11. Press RESET (red button) if needed to release pressure.  
12. **Slowly** open the press by releasing the hydraulic clamping pressure (per your specific press design — confirm with supervisor).  
13. Open the plate stack — pull plates apart one by one; filter cakes will fall (or need to be manually dislodged) into the collection bins below.  
14. Inspect each filter cloth — any torn or damaged cloths will allow sludge to bypass. Replace damaged cloths.  
15. Close plates, clamp, and repeat for next batch.

`⚠️ WARNING:` **Never open the plates while the press is under feed pump pressure.** The pressure will force liquid and sludge out explosively. Stop the feed pump and confirm pressure is zero before opening.

### Collection bins
Two IBC-style bins sit below the press to collect filter cakes. When full, arrange waste removal through a licensed waste contractor. Record: date, approximate volume/weight, waste type (iron-bearing sludge), contractor name and manifest number.

## 6.12 AODD Pump (Air-Operated Double-Diaphragm)

**Appearance:** Silver/aluminum body with two large circular end caps, air exhaust port in the centre, bolted flange connections. Labeled "OUT" with handwritten note.

**What it is:** An air-powered pump that uses two flexible diaphragms alternating back and forth to push liquid. No electric motor — runs on compressed air from the shop air supply.

**What it's used for:** Pumping thick sludge and slurries that would destroy a standard centrifugal pump. The AODD can handle:
- Wet cell sludge transfer to the filter press
- Sludge from the settling ponds
- Any thick, gritty slurry

### How to operate an AODD pump
`[ACTION]`  
1. Connect the suction hose to the inlet and the discharge hose to the outlet. Confirm all clamps are tight.  
2. Open the inlet valve (source) and outlet valve (destination).  
3. Connect the air supply hose to the air inlet port.  
4. Open the air supply valve — the pump will immediately start cycling with a characteristic "chunk-chunk-chunk" sound and air exhaust puffs.  
5. Increase air pressure to achieve the desired flow rate (more pressure = faster pumping, up to about 100 psi max).  
6. To stop: close the air supply valve.  
7. Disconnect in reverse order.

### Common problems
| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pump "stalls" (freezes mid-stroke) | Air pressure too low, or pilot valve worn/stuck | Increase air pressure; if stalled, quickly cycle the air pressure off/on to restart |
| Very slow, laboured pumping | Inlet blocked (suction hose kinked or solids blocking inlet) | Check and clear suction line |
| Air blowing out of discharge (not liquid) | Diaphragm failure | Take offline; replace diaphragm — have a spare on hand |
| Chattering/fast cycling but no flow | Air-locked (air in the inlet line) | Prime by tilting the pump or submerging the inlet |

## 6.15 CO₂ Cylinder Handling

**Cylinder hand truck:** Always use the dedicated hand truck to move the CO₂ cylinder. CO₂ cylinders are heavy (~50 kg full) and top-heavy.

`[ACTION]` When moving the CO₂ cylinder:
