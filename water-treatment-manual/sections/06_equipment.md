# Section 6 — Equipment Guide

> This section describes every major piece of equipment on this site — what it does, how to operate it, what normal looks like, and what warning signs to watch for. Learn the name and location of each piece before your first solo shift.

---

## 6.1 Outdoor Settling Ponds

**What they are:** Large concrete block-walled open basins outdoors.  
**There are two distinct ponds:**

| Pond | Typical water appearance | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary settling pond** | Turbid green or grey | First pond receiving raw site water. Heavy solids settle here. |
| **Stabilization / secondary pond** | Dark red or purple (iron-rich) | Receives more heavily contaminated water. Oil/water separation and additional settling. |

**What they do:** Gravity pre-settling. Given time (hours to days), large solids sink to the bottom, free oil floats to the surface, and the clearer middle layer is drawn off for further treatment.

`[CHECK]` Each shift:
- [ ] Water level in each pond — note if rising or falling
- [ ] Oil sheen on the surface — any visible oily rainbow or dark film?
- [ ] Debris (trash, vegetation) blocking the outlet pipe?
- [ ] Are the concrete block walls intact — any erosion or instability?

`ℹ️ WHY:` The settling ponds are the first treatment step and absorb the "shock loads" from rain events and site activity. If they're overloaded or short-circuiting, the rest of the system gets hammered.

## 6.2 Indoor Treatment / Storage Tanks (Large White Cylindrical Tanks)

**What they are:** Two large cylindrical above-ground tanks inside the treatment building. White poly or fibreglass construction, approximately 3–4 metres tall and 1.5–2 metres in diameter. Each has a metal ladder attached to the outside for level checking. Outlet piping and ball valves at the base. One tank is labelled "ANKO2" *(confirm exact tank IDs and capacity with supervisor)*.

**What they do:** These are the main indoor water storage or treatment vessels — likely the primary coagulation/flocculation contact tanks or holding tanks for water at different treatment stages. Water is pumped in, chemical dosing occurs, and the water moves on to the filters.

`[CHECK]` Each shift:
- [ ] Water level in each tank (visual from ladder or sight glass — *(confirm method)*)
- [ ] Tank status label current (clean / dirty / in treatment)
- [ ] No leaks at the base valves or outlet connections
- [ ] Chemical dosing lines are injecting into the correct tank

`ℹ️ WHY:` Mixing water from a "clean" tank into a "dirty" tank means retreating that water from scratch. Always know which tank contains what.

## 6.2a Wet Cell / Separation Cell (Indoor Concrete Settling Tanks)

**What they are:** Multi-compartment concrete tanks inside the building. Distinct from the large white cylindrical tanks — these are lower-profile concrete vessels with baffled partitions.  
**Appearance:** Heavy orange-brown iron staining on all walls and floor — this is normal, not neglect. It's the visual record of iron removal.

The baffled partitions divide the flow path into compartments, forcing water to travel a longer path and giving solids more time to settle.

**What they do:** Secondary settling after chemical dosing. Floc formed by PACl and polymer settles to the floor. The sludge accumulates here and must be periodically removed.

`[CHECK]` Each shift:
- [ ] Water level — is it at the normal operating level?
- [ ] Sludge on the floor — is it accumulating faster than usual? Can you see the bottom?
- [ ] Water clarity — does the outflow look noticeably clearer than the inflow?
- [ ] Any oil floating on top inside the wet cell?

**Sludge removal:** Pump the accumulated sludge from the bottom of the wet cell using the AODD pump (Section 6.12) to the filter press (Section 6.11) or to the stabilization pond as directed.

`⚠️ WARNING:` The wet cell is a **confined space**. Never enter without a confined space entry permit, atmospheric testing, and a trained attendant outside. The iron-laden environment can generate hydrogen gas and deplete oxygen. See Section 9.6.

## 6.3 Chemical Dosing Room

The chemical dosing room contains drums and dosing pumps for each treatment chemical. Each pump draws chemical from its drum and injects it into the water stream at the correct dose.

### PACl (Coagulant) Pump
- **Drum:** White 200L SUMAS drum, Class 8 label, "POLY ALUMINUM CHLORIDE"
- **Pump:** Blue diaphragm metering pump
- **Injection point:** Into the water flow before the wet cell (or as directed)

### CL-200 (Flocculant) Pump
- **Drum:** White drum, "NON-REGULATED MATERIAL — FLOCCULANT" label
- **Pump:** Yellow LMI diaphragm dosing pump
- **Injection point:** After PACl, before or during slow mixing in the wet cell

### Checking a dosing pump is actually working
`[CHECK]`  
1. Is the pump making a rhythmic clicking/ticking sound? (Diaphragm pump = audible with each stroke)  
2. Is the tank level dropping over the course of a shift?  
3. Is chemical visible at the injection point? (Hold a rag near the injection — you should see a small squirt/drip with each stroke)  
4. If the pump sounds right but nothing is coming out → check for a broken or cracked injection check valve, or a clogged injection point.

### Priming a dry dosing pump
If a pump has run dry or been disconnected, it needs priming:  
`[ACTION]`  
1. Disconnect the discharge tubing from the injection point (hold a rag over the end).  
2. Run the pump until liquid (not air bubbles) flows continuously from the discharge.  
3. Reconnect to the injection point.

`⚠️ WARNING:` Wear chemical-resistant gloves and splash goggles when working on any dosing pump — chemical may spray from the disconnected line.

## 6.4 Sand Filter (SRS-SF-11)

**Appearance:** Green cylindrical pressure vessel on a metal stand, with a pump mounted below/beside it. Tagged "SRS-SF-11".

**What it does:** Pressurized filtration through a bed of graded sand. Water flows down through the sand, which traps iron hydroxide particles, fine suspended solids, and any remaining floc.

`ℹ️ WHY:` After hydrogen peroxide oxidizes dissolved iron into iron hydroxide particles, those particles need to be removed. The sand filter is their primary removal point. The orange staining visible on the exterior of all equipment around the filter confirms how much iron this filter catches.

### Normal operation checks
`[CHECK]`  
- [ ] Water is flowing through (inlet pressure gauge reading)
- [ ] Outlet turbidity is lower than inlet turbidity
- [ ] No unusual vibration or noise from the pump below

### When does it need backwashing?
Backwash the sand filter when any of the following:
1. Outlet turbidity starts climbing above your target
2. Flow rate through the filter drops significantly (filter is clogged)
3. Pressure drop across the filter exceeds the setpoint

### Backwash procedure (generic — confirm exact valve positions with supervisor)
`[ACTION]`  
1. Close the inlet valve.  
2. Close the outlet (forward flow) valve.  
3. Open the backwash drain valve.  
4. Open the backwash supply valve (clean water in from the bottom, up through the sand).  
5. Run until the backwash discharge runs clear — typically 5–15 minutes.  
6. Close backwash valves, re-open inlet and outlet to return to normal operation.  
7. Redirect first 5–10 minutes of post-backwash effluent back to the wet cell (not forward to clean storage) — filter needs to "ripen" after backwash.  
8. Record backwash in the log: time, reason, duration.

## 6.5 Carbon Vessel (SRS-CV-15)

**Appearance:** Blue cylindrical pressure vessel, "PV SERIES" label, tagged "SRS-CV-15". Sits on a wooden pallet. **Maximum working pressure: 75 PSIG** (marked on the vessel — never exceed this).

**What it does:** Activated carbon adsorption. The vessel is filled with granular activated carbon (GAC) — a highly porous material with enormous surface area. Dissolved petroleum hydrocarbons, colour compounds, and odour-causing organics bind to the carbon surface as water passes through.

`ℹ️ WHY:` Suspended solids are removed by sand filtration. But dissolved hydrocarbons (the ones that give the water a fuel smell or create a downstream sheen) cannot be filtered — they pass through sand. Carbon adsorption is the only way to remove them.

### Normal operation checks
`[CHECK]`  
- [ ] Water is flowing through (inlet valve open, no unusual pressure)
- [ ] Outlet water does not smell of petroleum or fuel
- [ ] No colour in the effluent (should be essentially clear)

### Signs the carbon is exhausted (needs replacing)
- Petroleum odour returns in the treated water output
- Colour (brown/yellow tint) appears in the effluent
- The vessel has been in service for its rated operational volume (*(confirm with supervisor)* — carbon life depends on hydrocarbon loading)

`⚠️ WARNING:` Unlike a sand filter, you **cannot backwash carbon to restore capacity**. Once the carbon is saturated, the media must be physically removed and replaced with fresh carbon. Exhausted carbon is a regulated waste — dispose of through an approved waste contractor.

## 6.6 Bag Filter Housing

**Appearance:** Blue cylindrical filter housing, approximately 1 metre tall.  
**Filter bags:** Les Hall Filter brand
- Pre-filter bags: LHFSPE100PJNBW (100 micron) — removes larger particles
- Polishing bags: LHFSPE10PJNBW (10 micron) — removes fine particles

**What it does:** Final mechanical filtration step. Removes any particles that made it through the sand filter and carbon vessel. The 100-micron bag catches coarse particles; the 10-micron bag polishes the effluent.

### How to change a filter bag
`[ACTION]`  
1. **Close the inlet and outlet valves** on the bag filter housing.  
2. Open the **pressure relief/vent valve** to release pressure (there will be water in the housing — have a bucket ready).  
3. Undo the top closure ring and lift the lid.  
4. Pull the old bag out by the collar — hold it over a bucket, it will drip.  
5. Inspect the old bag: How much solids are on it? Is it iron-coloured or hydrocarbon-coloured? Note in the logbook.  
6. Place old bag in waste container.  
7. Install new bag — ensure the collar seats fully in the housing ring. A poorly seated collar allows water to bypass the bag entirely.  
8. Replace lid and tighten closure ring.  
9. Slowly open the inlet valve first (let housing fill), then open outlet.  
10. Check for leaks at the top closure.  
11. Record bag change: date, time, which bag (100-micron or 10-micron), appearance of old bag.

### When to change bags
- When flow rate through the housing drops noticeably (bag is loaded with solids)
- On a scheduled frequency *(confirm with supervisor — likely every X days or after treating X cubic metres)*
- After any event that puts unusual solids loading through the system (major rain event, wet cell cleanout)

## 6.7 Ozone System (AERQUS + CD30TIX)

**Equipment:**
- **CD30TIX Ozone Generation System** — stainless steel cabinet with pressure gauge. Generates ozone (O₃) from ambient air or oxygen supply.
- **AERQUS Ozone Diffuser** — black box. Distributes ozone into the water stream.

**What ozone does:**  
Ozone is a powerful oxidizer — much stronger than chlorine or hydrogen peroxide. In this system, it:
1. **Oxidizes remaining dissolved iron** — converts any Fe²⁺ that H₂O₂ didn't catch into Fe³⁺ particles for filtration
2. **Oxidizes dissolved organics and hydrocarbons** — breaks down petroleum compounds that carbon may have missed
3. **Disinfects** — kills bacteria and other pathogens

`ℹ️ WHY:` Ozone complements the H₂O₂ iron oxidation and carbon adsorption steps. It provides a final oxidation polish before chlorination. It also handles odour elimination — ozone destroys the molecules responsible for petroleum smell.

### Normal operation checks
`[CHECK]`  
- [ ] CD30TIX power indicator light is on
- [ ] Pressure gauge on CD30TIX reads in normal range *(confirm target with supervisor)*
- [ ] AERQUS diffuser is bubbling/working in the contact water
- [ ] Faint ozone smell in the room — this is normal and confirms ozone is being generated
- [ ] No alarm lights on either unit

### `⚠️ WARNING — Ozone is Toxic to Breathe`
- Ozone has a sharp, bleach-like smell. At low concentrations it irritates the respiratory tract. At higher concentrations it damages lungs.
- **Never smell ozone directly or put your face near the diffuser.**
- The room where ozone is used must be well-ventilated.
- **Workplace exposure limit (Canada):** 0.1 ppm (8-hour TWA) — very low
- If you smell strong ozone, ventilate the area and stand back.
- Ozone destroys rubber — inspect any rubber hoses or gaskets near the ozone system regularly for cracking/degradation.

## 6.8 Chlorination (Accu-Tab Blue Cal-Hypo Tablets)

**Product:** Accu-Tab Blue Calcium Hypochlorite tablets in a tablet chlorinator or contact vessel.

**What it does:** Final disinfection step before discharge. As water flows over the tablets, they dissolve slowly and release free chlorine. The target is a measurable free chlorine residual at the discharge point, confirming the water has been adequately disinfected.

### How to add tablets
`[ACTION]`  
1. Wear chemical splash goggles and chemical-resistant gloves.  
2. Confirm the contact vessel is dry or free of other chemical residues (especially H₂O₂ residue — see compatibility warning in Section 5.2).  
3. Add tablets as directed — do not add a full bucket at once if the vessel is not designed for it.  
4. Ensure water is flowing through at the correct rate (too fast = under-dosing; too slow = over-dosing).  
5. Test free chlorine in the effluent with a DPD test kit (see Section 4.4.2).  
6. Record: tablets added (approximate number or weight), test result, date, time.

### Tablet level monitoring
`[CHECK]` Each shift: check tablet level in the chlorinator. Tablets dissolve continuously. Running out = no disinfection = potential permit violation.

## 6.9 Oil/Water Separator (Shell Canada System)

**System:** Above-ground separator vessel + underground holding tank  
**Documentation:** Shell Canada log sheet and weekly inspection flow chart on file

**What it does:** Petroleum products (oil, fuel) are less dense than water, so they float. The separator allows water to sit long enough for:
- Petroleum to rise and accumulate at the top (where it is skimmed off)
- Sludge and heavy solids to sink to the bottom
- Cleaner water to be drawn from the middle layer

The recovered petroleum and sludge are **regulated wastes** — they must be drummed, labelled, and removed by a licensed waste contractor. They do NOT go to the discharge point.

### Weekly Inspection (per Shell Canada Flow Chart)

`[ACTION]` Each week:  
1. **Inspect the slab and drainage points** — any spills or leaks visible?  
2. **Dip the underground holding tank** for fuel thickness and sludge thickness.  
   - Record: total liquid depth, fuel thickness, sludge thickness  
3. **Check that liquid level is within acceptable range:** 0.8m to 1.1m from tank bottom (25,000 L = 0.8m, 44,000 L = 1.1m from bottom — *(confirm your tank specs)*)  
4. **Dip the above-ground separator** for fuel and sludge thickness.

### Action thresholds

| Measurement | Action |
|---|---|
| Underground tank sludge > 15 cm | Call to arrange sludge removal |
| Underground tank fuel (L.P. petroleum) > 2.5 cm | Arrange skimming |
| Above-ground separator fuel > 15 cm | Skim manually (call maintenance contractor if it re-occurs) |
| Liquid level outside 0.8–1.1m range | Check pump switch is set to automatic; call contractor if problem persists |
| Any visible petroleum bypass at outlet | Inspect separator; skim; notify Shell field engineer if re-occurs |

### Completing the Shell Canada log sheet
Every weekly inspection must be logged on the Shell Canada log sheet including:
- Date, location, inspector name
- Visual inspection results
- All dip measurements
- Any corrective actions taken
- Sludge/petroleum removed (litres)
- Contractor name and manifest number for any waste removal

## 6.10 Frac Tanks

**What they are:** Large portable above-ground steel water storage tanks — the same type used in oil-and-gas hydraulic fracturing ("frac") operations for water storage. Typically 100,000 L (100 m³) capacity each.

**Their role in this system:**
- Store raw site water before treatment
- Store treated water before discharge
- Used to transfer water between treatment stages
- Buffer capacity — allow you to treat in batches even when incoming flow is variable

### The clean/dirty rule
`⚠️ WARNING:` Mixing clean (treated) water with dirty (untreated) water means starting treatment over. Know the status of every frac tank at all times.

`[ACTION]` Maintain a visible label or tag on each frac tank:
- **DIRTY / UNTREATED** — raw site water
- **IN TREATMENT** — water currently being processed
- **CLEAN / TREATED** — water that has passed all treatment steps and is ready for discharge or reuse

### Level monitoring
- Check levels each shift — manual dip tape or sight glass *(confirm method with supervisor)*
- Record in logbook: tank ID, level, content status (clean/dirty)
- Alert supervisor if: a clean tank is getting dangerously low (need to treat more); a dirty tank is overflowing (need to treat faster or manage incoming flow)

### Transferring between frac tanks (per "Brennan's List" task note)
`[ACTION]`  
1. Confirm source and destination tanks and their current status.  
2. Connect transfer hose — check for cracks, leaks, loose fittings before starting.  
3. Start transfer pump; monitor for leaks at all connections.  
4. When source tank is at the desired level, stop the pump and disconnect hoses.  
5. Update tank labels and log the transfer.

## 6.11 Kontek APC5 Filter Press (Sludge Dewatering)

**Manufacturer:** Kontek Ecology Systems Inc., 3250 Harvester Road Unit #1, Burlington, Ontario L7N 3W8  
**Phone:** (905) 332-8365 | www.kontekecology.com  
**Model:** APC5 | **Serial:** K2012190 | **Electrical:** 120V, 1-phase, 3A

**What it does:** A filter press squeezes water out of sludge, producing:
1. **Filter cakes** — semi-dry solids that fall into collection bins below the press. These are regulated waste — removed by licensed contractor.
2. **Filtrate water** — the water pressed out of the sludge. Usually returned to the head of the treatment system for re-treatment.

`ℹ️ WHY:` The wet cell and settling ponds accumulate sludge continuously. You cannot just pump sludge to a drain — it must be dewatered and disposed of as regulated waste. The filter press is how you turn a liquid sludge into a manageable solid waste.

### Control Panel
- **Keyed ON/OFF switch** (top) — must be in the ON (I) position for the press to operate
- **Green START button** (middle) — starts the hydraulic pump / pressing cycle
- **Red RESET button** (bottom) — stops the cycle / resets after a fault

### Basic Operation Sequence
`[ACTION]`  
**Before starting:**  
1. Confirm all filter plates are properly closed and aligned.  
2. Confirm the hydraulic ram has clamped the plate stack tight (check that there is no gap between plates).  
3. Place collection bins beneath the press to catch filter cakes.  
4. Confirm filtrate collection is set up (where will the pressed water go?).

**Running the press:**  
5. Key switch to ON.  
6. Press the green START button.  
7. Start the sludge feed pump — pump the sludge slurry into the press inlet. The press fills from one end and fills each chamber between plates.  
8. Monitor the filtrate outlets on each plate — you should see water draining from each plate's corner outlets.  
9. The press cycle is complete when: filtrate flow slows to almost nothing (press is full, all water has been squeezed out).  
10. Stop the sludge feed pump.  
11. Press RESET (red button) if needed to release pressure.  
12. **Slowly** open the press by releasing the hydraulic clamping pressure (per your specific press design — confirm with supervisor).  
13. Open the plate stack — pull plates apart one by one; filter cakes will fall (or need to be manually dislodged) into the collection bins below.  
14. Inspect each filter cloth — any torn or damaged cloths will allow sludge to bypass. Replace damaged cloths.  
15. Close plates, clamp, and repeat for next batch.

`⚠️ WARNING:` **Never open the plates while the press is under feed pump pressure.** The pressure will force liquid and sludge out explosively. Stop the feed pump and confirm pressure is zero before opening.

### Collection bins
Two IBC-style bins sit below the press to collect filter cakes. When full, arrange waste removal through a licensed waste contractor. Record: date, approximate volume/weight, waste type (iron-bearing sludge), contractor name and manifest number.

## 6.12 AODD Pump (Air-Operated Double-Diaphragm)

**Appearance:** Silver/aluminum body with two large circular end caps, air exhaust port in the centre, bolted flange connections. Labeled "OUT" with handwritten note.

**What it is:** An air-powered pump that uses two flexible diaphragms alternating back and forth to push liquid. No electric motor — runs on compressed air from the shop air supply.

**What it's used for:** Pumping thick sludge and slurries that would destroy a standard centrifugal pump. The AODD can handle:
- Wet cell sludge transfer to the filter press
- Sludge from the settling ponds
- Any thick, gritty slurry

### How to operate an AODD pump
`[ACTION]`  
1. Connect the suction hose to the inlet and the discharge hose to the outlet. Confirm all clamps are tight.  
2. Open the inlet valve (source) and outlet valve (destination).  
3. Connect the air supply hose to the air inlet port.  
4. Open the air supply valve — the pump will immediately start cycling with a characteristic "chunk-chunk-chunk" sound and air exhaust puffs.  
5. Increase air pressure to achieve the desired flow rate (more pressure = faster pumping, up to about 100 psi max).  
6. To stop: close the air supply valve.  
7. Disconnect in reverse order.

### Common problems
| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pump "stalls" (freezes mid-stroke) | Air pressure too low, or pilot valve worn/stuck | Increase air pressure; if stalled, quickly cycle the air pressure off/on to restart |
| Very slow, laboured pumping | Inlet blocked (suction hose kinked or solids blocking inlet) | Check and clear suction line |
| Air blowing out of discharge (not liquid) | Diaphragm failure | Take offline; replace diaphragm — have a spare on hand |
| Chattering/fast cycling but no flow | Air-locked (air in the inlet line) | Prime by tilting the pump or submerging the inlet |

## 6.13 pH Meter and Lab Setup

**Equipment visible on site:**
- Portable pH meter (grey hard-sided case)
- pH buffer solutions: pH 4.0 (red), pH 7.0 (yellow), pH 10.0 (blue)
- Sample jars
- PVC primer (IPEX 5) and cement (Oatey) for plumbing repairs

### pH meter calibration (before each use)
`[ACTION]`  
1. Remove the pH probe from its storage solution. Rinse with distilled or clean water.  
2. Blot dry (do not rub).  
3. Place in pH 7.0 buffer. Press CAL. Let it stabilize. Confirm it reads 7.0 ± 0.05.  
4. Rinse electrode, blot dry.  
5. Place in pH 4.0 buffer (for acidic water testing). Press CAL. Let stabilize. Confirm reads 4.0 ± 0.05.  
6. Rinse and blot dry again. Meter is ready to use.  
7. After use: rinse electrode, store in pH electrode storage solution (not distilled water — distilled water damages the electrode).

## 6.15 CO₂ Cylinder Handling

**Cylinder hand truck:** Always use the dedicated hand truck to move the CO₂ cylinder. CO₂ cylinders are heavy (~50 kg full) and top-heavy.

`[ACTION]` When moving the CO₂ cylinder:
1. Close the cylinder valve completely.
2. Attach the protective cap to the valve.
3. Tilt the cylinder onto the hand truck. Secure the chain or strap around the cylinder.
4. Tilt the hand truck back and wheel to the new location.
5. Secure the cylinder to the wall bracket or fixed support before removing from the hand truck.

`⚠️ WARNING:` **Never move a CO₂ cylinder without the hand truck.** A falling cylinder can rupture the valve — the cylinder becomes a pressurized projectile. Also: CO₂ cylinders must always be chained or strapped to a fixed support when not on the hand truck.
